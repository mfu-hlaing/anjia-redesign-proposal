Ownership

What a foreigner may lawfully hold in Thailand.

A foreigner may not own Thai land outright. That single sentence is where most agents stop, and it is nowhere near the end of the subject. There are three established routes, each with a different price, a different resale, and a different set of documents. Reading this before you look at a listing will save you a great deal of time.

The three routes compared
RouteInterest heldTerm Applies toThe constraint
Freehold condominiumOutright, in your own namePerpetual Condominium units only49% foreign quota per building

01

Freehold condominium, and the 49% that decides everything

Under the Condominium Act, a foreign national may own a condominium unit outright — the same title, the same rights, the same perpetual term as a Thai buyer. This is the cleanest form of ownership available to you in Thailand, and it is why the collection is weighted towards condominiums.

The limit is at the building level, not yours. No more than 49% of a building's total saleable floor area may be held in foreign names. A building that has reached its quota cannot register another foreign owner, however much you offer — you either wait for an existing foreign owner to sell their unit, or you take a different route to the same apartment.

This is the single most useful thing to establish before you fall in love with a floor plan, and it is the reason the first filter on our collection is tenure rather than price. Where a listing here says Foreign quota, that unit currently sits inside the 49% and can be transferred into your own name.

The money has to arrive from abroad.
To register foreign-quota ownership the purchase funds must be remitted into Thailand in foreign currency and converted on arrival. The receiving bank issues a Foreign Exchange Transaction record — the FET form, sometimes called a credit advice — and the Land Office will ask for it at transfer. Money already sitting in a Thai account, or remitted in baht, creates a problem that is tedious to unwind. We set this up before you transfer anything.

02

Registered leasehold, for anything with land under it

A villa, a townhouse, a detached house — anything where you are really buying the plot — cannot be held freehold by a foreigner. The established route is a lease registered against the title at the Land Office, for a maximum term of thirty years.

Leases are commonly written with renewal options, often described as "30 + 30" or "30 + 30 + 30". It is important to understand what that is: the first thirty years are a registered property right; the renewals are contractual promises by the current owner. They are ordinarily honoured, and they are not the same thing as title. The drafting of the renewal clause, the succession provisions, and what happens if the freeholder sells are where the value of good counsel actually sits.

Anything under three years need not be registered to be valid; a thirty-year term must be registered at the Land Office to bind anyone but the original parties. Register it.

03

Sap-Ing-Sith, the newer instrument

Thailand's Right over Property Act created a registrable right — Sap-Ing-Sith — that sits between a lease and ownership. It runs for a term of up to thirty years, it is registered against the title, and unlike an ordinary lease it is transferable and can be used as security for a loan. It also survives the sale of the underlying land, and passes to heirs.

For a foreign buyer those three properties — transferable, mortgageable, inheritable — make it materially stronger than a lease. The trade is novelty: it is still uncommon in practice, fewer lawyers have drafted one, and the resale market for such rights is thin. It is worth asking about, and worth taking advice on, rather than assuming.

What a transfer actually costs

Who pays what is negotiable, and it is negotiated badly.
The convention in Pattaya is that transfer fees are split equally between buyer and seller, with the seller carrying the taxes. It is a convention, not a rule — and on a new development the developer's standard contract frequently allocates all of it to you. It is worth reading before you sign, which is a sentence we would rather you heard from us than learned later.

Before you commit

What we check, on every residence, before it reaches the collection

Title

The chanote

Title deed examined at the Land Office: that the seller is the registered owner, that the boundaries are what the plan says, and that no mortgage, servitude or other encumbrance is registered against it.

Quota

The 49%

Current foreign-ownership position confirmed with the juristic person in writing, and the specific unit's status established — not the building's status in general, which is where this usually goes wrong.

Standing

The building's finances

Sinking fund balance, arrears, outstanding levies and any litigation the juristic person is party to. A beautiful building with an empty sinking fund becomes an expensive one.
